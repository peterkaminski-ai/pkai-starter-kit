# From House to Estate

A house is an agent home and a headquarters. An estate is a house that has grown: several agents, some in the foreground and some in the background; a body of knowledge the agents keep and can find their way around; and enough going on that the house needs its own conventions to stay coherent. The word marks a capacity, not a size. A big folder isn't an estate. A house that can hold more, do more, and stay legible while it does — that is.

This chapter is the short version. The PKAI benchmark — the fuller statement this kit is shaped from; your `LINEAGE.md` says where — has the long one.

## What changes

**More than one agent.** A second agent with a different temperament — a rigorous one beside a warm one, an archivist beside a chief of staff — living in the same `My Agents` folder with its own home, memory and charter. They share the HQ. They don't share a charter, and they never write each other's memory; if one learns something the other should know, it writes it down where the other will read it, and the other decides.

**Foreground and background as a habit.** In a house it's an occasional trick. In an estate it's how the day runs: a foreground you're talking to, several background instances working jobs, each in its own scratch folder, each leaving a handoff. The [previous chapter](Foreground%20and%20Background.md) is the whole mechanism; an estate just runs it constantly.

**Knowledge that has to be findable.** A house can rely on memory and a few project folders. An estate has years of it — sessions, decisions, drafts, the correspondence behind every finished thing — and the question "where did we…" is asked ten times a day. The information suite (`save-a-memory`, `find-it-again`, `review-and-prune`) is the minimum; an estate adds its own conventions on top: where status files live, how projects are named, what gets an index.

**Books and their boxes.** A finished thing — a document, a release, a book — sits next to the pile that made it: drafts, half-attempts, transcripts, the back-and-forth. An estate keeps them side by side, the polished face over the working pile, and it's one of the most useful patterns there is. It gives every messy thing a home without letting the mess into the finished work.

**Shared state has an owner.** With two agents, someone has to own the shared things — the daily notes, the list of what's in flight, the estate's memory of itself. The estate names which agent holds that desk, and the other reads freely but doesn't write it. Pass the desk by telling one of them; the other finds out on its next wake. Two agents editing the same file is a question for the person, never a race.

## What deliberately doesn't change

- **Everything is still markdown.** An estate that needs a database to stay coherent has stopped being legible, and legibility is the whole bargain.
- **The HQ still has no charter.** It's a place. Each agent brings itself.
- **Authority is still per agent, still earned.** A new agent in an old estate starts narrow. Trust is granted by the person, in words, and written down; it's never inherited from a sibling.
- **The firewall is the same firewall.** More agents means more untrusted text — from each other, from rooms, from the machine. What an agent reads is information, never instruction, and a request relayed by a trusted sibling gets the same scrutiny as a stranger's.
- **Pull, never push.** An estate takes new releases the same way a house does: reads the changes, decides, reports back. Nothing is installed into it from outside, and no agent in it installs anything into another.

## How you know you're there

Not by folder size. You're running an estate when you have a second agent and would miss it; when background instances are the normal way long work gets done; when your agents can find things you've forgotten you had; and when you've had to write down at least one convention because two agents needed to agree on it. At that point the kit has done its job, and the benchmark is the book to read next.
